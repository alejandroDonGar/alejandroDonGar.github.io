ZeeBoard

A desktop app for artists who work on commission. It follows each drawing from the request until it's paid.

My role
The idea, the design and the testing; the code, with AI
Since
June 2026, ongoing
Stack
Tauri, Rust, React, TypeScript and SQLite
Status
Version 0.2, installable and in daily use

Context

The problem

In my free time I draw on the iPad and take commissions. I open slots on Twitter and Bluesky, people request their character through a Google form with their references, and each drawing goes through stages: sketch, lineart, colour. The client reviews each one and sometimes asks for changes before I move on.

I kept all of that spread across the form, PayPal, image folders and notes. Checking where a drawing was or whether someone had paid meant looking in four places, so I wanted a board that showed it at a glance, without uploading my clients' data to any service.

My role

The idea came from a need of my own. I don't know Rust or Tauri and only a little TypeScript, so almost all of the code is written by an AI assistant (Claude Code) from what I ask for. I did it that way to have something working quickly.

My part is deciding what the app does and how it looks, testing every change with my real commissions, and reading the code until I understand what it does. The work happens on branches, with small commits and a plan that gets ticked off. It's at 152 commits today.

Decisions

Everything lives on the computer

The data belongs to my clients: names, PayPal emails and what they've paid me. That's why ZeeBoard is a desktop app with Tauri and a local SQLite database, with no server and no accounts.

What you lose is cloud backup, so the app makes a backup every day in a folder of your choice (I use a Google Drive one) and can restore it without deleting anything.

50 MB images that don't slow the app down

My exported canvases are 40 to 70 MB each. At first the app stored them as they were inside the database and everything was slow. Now, when you import an image, Rust makes a WebP copy of at most 2560 px and a 480 px thumbnail. The original stays on the iPad.

From the iPad to the stage, no cables

I draw on the iPad and the computer is wired, so they aren't on the same network. The app runs a small Rust server, and Tailscale connects both devices even when the iPad is on mobile data.

From a key-protected page that opens with a QR code, I send the image straight to the commission's stage. Client corrections are marked with pins on the image.

What I learned

Not to touch anyone's data without a safety net: every migration saves a copy of the database first, and 15 check scripts verify payments, imports and the trash every time I change something.

And to read code in languages I don't know well: understanding what each part does even if I didn't write it, and knowing what to ask for when something breaks.

What I'd do differently: keep the images out of the database from day one. Moving them later meant converting the data I already had.
